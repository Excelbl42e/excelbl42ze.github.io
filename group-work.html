Reflection: Working in a Group

Communication and Study Skills (SCIS111) group presentation, October 2026 · Framework: Tuckman's stages of group development

Introduction

This semester I worked in Group 1.1. Our group was put together through the module's group allocation, completed by 18 September 2026, and we were added to our group on Microsoft Teams. There were six of us: me and five classmates, Michael Chitonho, Medo Bathaedi, Sibusiso Tshuma, Theetso (Bafo) Baforegi and Maatla Balo. Our task was to run a simulated Student Board meeting and deliver a 10-minute presentation, with a separate Q&A of about three minutes, proposing a First-Year Student Success Plan for BAC. I was the chairperson, which meant leading the project and the meeting itself. Using Tuckman's model, I look back at how our group developed and what I learnt.

Forming

I already knew all of my group mates before the project, because we are in the same class, so we did not have to break the ice the way a group of strangers would. At the start, the main job was deciding who would do what. As chairperson, I made sure the work was divided equally: every member was given two objectives and their own slides. Michael became the secretary, responsible for recording our team process and deadlines. I chose Medo as the proposer because her objective, the solutions, matched the proposer role. Maatla researched the challenges first-year students face, and Sibusiso, Bafo and Michael covered the overview, purpose and team process. My own part was Slide 9, a practical communication demonstration, and Slide 12, the group dynamics reflection, which I shared with Sibusiso. We set up a WhatsApp group to coordinate.

Storming

We did not have big arguments, and I think the main reason is that roles were clear from the beginning. Because everyone owned specific slides, nobody could sit back and let others do the work, which prevents social loafing. Our real challenge was scheduling. We planned a progress check for Thursday 1 October, but some members could not attend. Instead of going ahead without them, we compromised and moved it to Saturday 3 October so that everyone could take part. Another moment I remember is when our presentation script contained a line suggesting Bafo spoke less than the others. I felt it was unfair to him and had it removed, because everyone's contribution deserved to be recognised equally.

Norming

Once roles were settled, we agreed on our slides, our deadlines and how the presentation would run. Members started supporting each other beyond their own parts: Bafo helped Michael with the Team Process Record, and Sibusiso helped me with the reflection slide. Having a chairperson and a secretary kept us organised, because everyone got a say and we had a record of what we had decided.

Performing

We reached the performing stage when we built and practised the full presentation together, including working side by side in the computer lab. Our slides alternated between problems and solutions, so Maatla and Medo's sections had to connect smoothly. In my demonstration, I compared a lecturer posting a deadline change in a busy WhatsApp group, where it gets buried under chatter, with the same change sent as a clear Outlook email that students must acknowledge. As chairperson I also ran the agenda, handed over between speakers and led the Q&A.

Adjourning

After presenting, we submitted our work with an acknowledgement confirming that every member had contributed. Our group closed without unresolved tension, partly because the workload had been shared fairly and the scheduling problem had been settled by compromise rather than left to grow.

What I learned about communication and teamwork

First, clear roles from the start are the best way to prevent both conflict and unequal participation. When everyone knows exactly what they own, there is less to argue about and nowhere to hide. Second, a good team adjusts to its members: moving one meeting cost us two days but kept the whole group involved. Third, I noticed an irony in our own process. Our key message was that important information should go through a proper, recorded channel, yet we coordinated mostly through WhatsApp, the same informal channel we criticised. It worked for a small group of six, but it showed me how easily important messages can get lost. In future group work and in the workplace, I will agree roles and meeting times at the first meeting, keep a written record of decisions, and put deadlines and key decisions in a channel that cannot be buried.
